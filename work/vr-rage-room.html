VR Rage Room

My MSc dissertation: a room full of things to smash, in VR or on a flat screen, built to compare how people feel after each.

my role
Solo: design, programming, testing and every 3D model (made in Blender)
engine
Unity, with the XR Interaction Toolkit
platform
VR headset and flatscreen (dual mode)
context
MSc Computer Games Technology dissertation, City St George's, University of London
playtest
8 players, each played both versions
year
2024

The idea

A rage room simulation you can play in VR or on a normal screen. The point wasn't only to make breaking things feel good: it was a research tool. The question was whether VR changes how people feel after letting off steam, and whether better physics makes a difference, so the same room had to work in both modes.

Before building anything I looked at how real rage rooms are set up: what people smash, what they smash it with, and the axe-throwing lanes that often sit next door. That turned into a hand-drawn floor plan with six zones.

The room

  • Break Zone: pick up plates and glasses and throw them onto the spiked mat, or smash them with a hammer
  • Punching bag: a soft-body bag in the middle of the room that dents when you hit it
  • Knockdown: throw balls at a stack of breakable boxes
  • Axe Throw: aim for the bullseye
  • Ice Sculpting: slice things in two with a sword, or trim the edges with a chipper
  • Chaos zone: breakable and soft-body objects all together, to do as you please

Before the main room there's a small demo room for learning the controls. When you're ready, you slot a cube into the 'Provide Key' socket and the wall opens.

What I built

  • Slicing: the sword cuts an object in two along the line of your swing. I added a minimum-volume check so pieces can't be cut forever, and wrote the chipper, which only trims small slivers when you move slowly
  • Soft bodies: the punching bag, plus a sloped object that acts like a trampoline when you toss plates onto it. I added impact sound that gets louder and changes pitch the harder it's dented
  • Fracture: plates, glasses and blocks break into chunks when they're hit hard enough
  • A flat-screen version of the same room, with keyboard and mouse controls and swing animations for the hammer and sword
  • Every 3D model in the room, kept low-poly and made in Blender
  • The playtest: questionnaires and screen recordings for both versions

The hardest part: getting the physics right

Making things deform and break convincingly was the hardest part, and it all had to run in VR. Dropped frames in a headset are uncomfortable, so the target was a stable 90 FPS. I got there on mid-tier hardware through custom collision mesh design.

Lighting mattered too. I swapped 14 real-time point lights for over 20 baked area lights, tuned Unity's physics settings and put objects on collision layers so things only collide with what they should.

What I'd do differently

Split it into different rooms, each built around a different kind of physics, instead of having one room handle everything. Each room could then be tuned on its own.

I'd also merge the physics scripts. Slicing, soft bodies and fracture are three separate systems, so a sliced object can't then shatter. Players noticed the game could still stutter when lots of things broke at once, and one asked to be able to smash plates against each other.

How it turned out

Eight people played both versions. VR scored 4.38 out of 5 overall, ahead of the flat screen, and seven of the eight spent longer in VR. Slicing was the favourite. The axe throw turned out too hard: one player kept fetching the axes for another go.

The project was part of an MSc I finished with a Distinction (71.53%). Holding VR scenes at 90 FPS is now part of my job at Candela.

The slicing, soft-body and fracture code started from open-source work (the EzySlice library and a Valem tutorial, Unity-SoftBody-physics, and Unity Libre Fracture). My part was adapting and extending it and building the game around it.
